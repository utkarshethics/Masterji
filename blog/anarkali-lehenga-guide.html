Anarkali & Lehenga Tailoring Guide: Flairs, Kalis, and Perfect Margins

By Masterji Expert Tailors | Published: July 2026 | Category: Ethnic Wear Hub

When it comes to grand Indian weddings and festivals, Anarkali suits and Lehengas are the undisputed queens of ethnic fashion. However, these garments are incredibly complex to stitch. They require vast amounts of heavy fabric, intricate embroidery matching, and precise structural engineering to ensure the garment flares beautifully without overwhelming the wearer.

At masterji.online, our master tailors possess decades of experience handling delicate silks, heavy zardozi work, and voluminous tulles. In this guide, we dive deep into the technical secrets behind flawless Anarkali and Lehenga tailoring, and why an expert cut makes all the difference.

1. The Geometry of the "Kali" (Panels)

The defining feature of a traditional Anarkali or a panelled Lehenga is the Kali—a vertical panel of fabric that is narrow at the top (waist) and flares out widely at the bottom (hem). The number of kalis dictates the volume and flow of the garment.

  • 8 to 12 Kalis: Provides a moderate, elegant A-line flare. Ideal for lighter fabrics like georgette or chiffon.
  • 16 to 24 Kalis (or more): Creates a dramatic, voluminous umbrella effect. Often used for heavy bridal lehengas or royal Anarkalis.

The challenge for a master tailor is to ensure that the waist measurement is divided perfectly among all the kalis. A miscalculation of even 0.25 inches per kali on a 16-kali lehenga results in a 4-inch error at the waist, completely ruining the fit. Custom tailoring ensures mathematical precision based on your exact high-waist measurements.

2. Managing the Can-Can (Volumizing Net)

To achieve the signature "princess" flare in a lehenga or a heavy Anarkali, tailors sew layers of stiff net, known as "Can-Can," inside the lining. However, improperly placed Can-Can is a recipe for disaster.

If Can-Can is stitched starting directly from the waist, it adds unnecessary bulk to the hips, making the wearer look wider. A skilled master tailor will start attaching the Can-Can layers roughly mid-thigh or knee-level. This maintains a sleek, fitted silhouette around the hips while providing a dramatic flare at the bottom hem.

3. Waistbands: The Drawstring vs. Side Zipper Dilemma

Lehenga skirts traditionally utilized heavy drawstrings (naada). While authentic, drawstrings create bunching at the front waist, which looks lumpy under a fitted blouse.

Modern bespoke tailoring solves this by engineering a structured flat front waistband with an elasticized back, or a completely flat band utilizing a concealed side-zipper and hook closure. This provides a clean, seamless look around the midriff. Additionally, our tailors reinforce the waistband with fusing to ensure it does not fold or roll down under the heavy weight of the skirt.

4. The Importance of Structural Margins

Ethnic wear is an investment, often passed down or reworn over many years. Therefore, leaving adequate margins inside the garment is non-negotiable.

  • Side Seam Margins: We leave at least 2 inches of fabric on either side of the Anarkali bodice.
  • Hemline Folds: For growing teenagers or for flexibility with different heel heights, we utilize a deep fold at the hemline rather than a narrow trim.
  • Embroidery Protection: When altering or stitching heavy zardozi or stonework fabric, our tailors painstakingly remove the stones along the seam line before stitching. This prevents the sewing machine needle from breaking and ensures the stones don't scratch the wearer's skin on the inside.
